# How I actually work with AI agents

Most people use AI the way they use a search box. They ask a question, get an answer, and walk away. That is not an agent. An agent, the way I use the word, is someone who can accomplish an objective, who can build you something, or who can repeat something every day on a scheduled job. Someone you can actually rely on.

I run my work with a team of them. This is how, and what it took to get there.

## It started with catching my own mistakes

In the spring of 2026 I was working in regular AI chats, and I noticed something. Every time I asked for a piece of work to be verified or reviewed, the review caught something. Every time. And the flip side was worse: when the same chat that wrote the work reviewed its own work, it stamped it fine. I wrote that failure down in my own log in plain words: producing-mode self-review rubber-stamps its own error.

So I built the fix into the system instead of hoping for better answers. I made a verify skill and a review skill, so checking became a separate job done with fresh eyes. I made a rule that every claim in a serious document has to be tied to a source that was actually opened, and marked when it was not. That discipline grew. The hooks, the review steps, the checks that run before anything is allowed to call itself finished: that is where my consulting system came from. It was born from catching errors, not from a business plan.

## How the operation runs today

The work lives in specialized project folders, one per kind of work. On top of those folders sit different agent harnesses, and I choose between them the way you would choose between people: by what each one can actually reach and do. One harness is where extensive building happens. Another is my orchestrator: it plans, it runs background chats in parallel, and it reaches the accounts and services the builder cannot. The agents also talk to each other directly, in a channel I can read, so work passes between them without me carrying messages.

Today, I decide where a task goes, by capability. I am still learning what each harness does best. But the direction is set: every time I learn something about what works, I write it down as an instruction or a rule, and the system needs me a little less. Autonomy in my setup is not one big switch. It is granted lane by lane, where the machinery has proven itself. One lane already runs with full autonomy, corresponding with recruiters and filing applications inside rules I set, because that machinery earned it.

## The part most people skip: testing

A lot of people get caught building. They build and build and never test, and they never arrive at the right result. In my system, the tests live inside the skills themselves. A skill gets run about ten times. I measure how well the model followed the instructions. Then I adjust, slowly. That is the difference between a demo and something you can hand real work to.

That is also how I decide what to trust. I do not trust an output because it looks right. I trust it when I know the mechanisms behind it work. I rely on my family dashboard every day because I know exactly how it is built and I know it is accurate. I ignore my daily brief, because I know some of the jobs feeding it are broken. Same screen, same kind of output. The difference is what is underneath.

## What this means for a business

People ask where a business should start with this. Not with a big knowledge base, and not with ten automations at once. Start by fixing one problem workflow. Get it working. Once it works, its output has a place to land, and that is what feeds the company brain. The brain grows out of workflows that actually run. Then the owner can ask it questions and get answers that hold together, the way my agent system pulls a real estate agent's scattered apps into one dashboard they never had before.

Automate without that, and you get the classic result: the left arm does not talk to the right arm. The knowledge underneath is what makes the pieces one operation instead of a pile of tools.

## Where it is going

Right now the whole setup is being rebuilt on one always-on machine, with GitHub as the hub every piece of work moves through, and one shared brain that every harness connects to. Same doctrine, stronger foundation: one brain, many hands, everything tested, and nothing trusted until the machinery behind it has earned it.
